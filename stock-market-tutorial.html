Prologue: Before You Risk Money

Welcome to Stock.Market_Education_Hub

This course is written for someone starting from absolute zero. The goal is not to turn you into a gambler with more indicators. It is to help you understand what you own, how markets work, how to read information, and how to control risk.

How to use this guide

Go in order. For every chapter, first understand the idea, then work through the example, then answer the “check your understanding” question without searching for the answer.

Important: This is educational material, not personalized financial advice. Markets involve loss of capital. Learn, paper trade, backtest, and understand local rules before using real money.

The rule for this course

Learn first → observe second → paper trade third → risk real money last. We will not judge progress by how much money you make in one week. We will judge it by whether you can explain why a trade exists, what would prove it wrong, and how much you could lose.

1. Introduction to the Stock Market: What Are You Actually Buying?

We live in a world where almost everyone has heard sentences such as: "The stock market crashed today," "This stock went up 10%," or "My friend doubled his money in stocks." Then one day you open a stock-market app, see a screen full of red and green numbers, strange graphs, abbreviations, flashing prices, and percentages... and quietly close the app again. 😅

If that sounds familiar, don't worry. The stock market looks complicated mainly because beginners are often introduced to the numbers before they are introduced to the idea.

So, before learning about candlesticks, RSI, MACD, moving averages, P/E ratios, support, resistance, stop losses, or anything that sounds remotely terrifying, we need to answer one very simple question:

What exactly is a stock?

The Basic Idea: A Company Can Be Divided Into Pieces

Imagine that you start a small bubble-tea company called Happy Bubble Tea Ltd. 🧋

Your first shop becomes extremely popular. To scale up, you plan to:

  • Open more branches
  • Buy better machines
  • Hire more employees
  • Advertise your brand
  • Develop new products
  • Expand internationally

Your business has incredible potential, but there is one tiny problem:
You need money. A lot of money. 💰

Suppose you divide the ownership of your entire company into 1,000,000 equal units.

Each of these ownership units is called a Share.

If somebody owns one of these shares, that person owns a very small ownership interest in the company.

Suppose an investor owns 10,000 shares. In our simplified example, their ownership percentage is:

$$\text{Ownership Percentage} = \frac{\text{Shares You Own}}{\text{Total Shares}} \times 100= \frac{10,000}{1,000,000} \times 100 = 1\%$$

So, in this simplified example, that investor owns approximately 1% of the company's shares.

A share represents an ownership interest in a company.

This is the first idea you should permanently keep in your mind. A stock is not simply a green or red number moving on your phone. Behind that number is a real business.

Stock vs Share: Are They the Same Thing?

You will hear the words stock and share constantly throughout this course.

In everyday conversation, people often use them interchangeably, but there is a small difference.

Stock

Usually refers more generally to ownership in a company.

Example: "I own TSMC stock."

Share

Refers to an individual unit of ownership.

Example: "I own 100 shares."

You do not need to obsess over this distinction. Even experienced investors casually use the terms interchangeably.

Shares = Small units of company ownership

Then What Is the "Stock Market"?

Now imagine there are thousands of companies. Some investors want to buy their shares. Other investors already own shares and want to sell them. We therefore need an organized system where buyers and sellers can meet. That organized system is what we broadly call the stock market.

🥬 Think About a Vegetable Market

One person wants to sell tomatoes. Another person wants to buy tomatoes. They meet in a marketplace where buyers and sellers interact and agree on prices. The stock market follows the same fundamental idea, except instead of tomatoes, potatoes, or fish, people are exchanging ownership interests in companies.

Modern financial markets are obviously much more sophisticated. Most transactions occur electronically, and buyers and sellers usually never meet each other physically.

But underneath all the computers, algorithms, financial terminology, and flashing numbers, the fundamental mechanism remains surprisingly simple:

Somebody wants to buy. Somebody wants to sell. The market tries to match them.

The "Online Marketplace" Analogy: Understanding Bid and Ask 🛒

Before learning how stock prices move, we need to understand two extremely important words: Bid and Ask.

These two prices represent what buyers and sellers are currently willing to do in the market.

BID — What the Buyer Offers

The bid price is the highest price a buyer is currently willing to pay.

"I am willing to BUY at NT$28,000."

ASK — What the Seller Wants

The ask price is the lowest price a seller is currently willing to accept.

"I am willing to SELL at NT$30,000."

Example: You Want to Buy a Laptop 💻

Imagine that someone is selling a laptop in an online marketplace.

The seller says:

Seller: "I will sell my laptop for NT$30,000."

In stock-market language, NT$30,000 is the Ask.

But you do not want to pay that much. You respond:

Buyer: "I am willing to pay NT$28,000."

In stock-market language, NT$28,000 is the Bid.

No Trade Yet

Buyer is willing to pay: NT$28,000

Seller wants: NT$30,000

Because the two sides do not agree on price, no transaction occurs yet.

The Difference Between Bid and Ask: The Spread

The difference between the best available Ask and the best available Bid is called the Bid–Ask Spread.

BID
NT$28,000
↔
ASK
NT$30,000

Spread = NT$30,000 − NT$28,000 = NT$2,000

In real stock markets, the spread is usually much smaller than this laptop example. We are using a large difference only to make the idea easy to see.

So When Does the Trade Actually Happen?

A transaction occurs when a buyer and seller become willing to trade at compatible prices.

Suppose the seller lowers the asking price:

Seller initially asks: NT$30,000
↓
Seller lowers Ask to: NT$29,000

At the same time, suppose you increase your offer:

Buyer initially bids: NT$28,000
↓
Buyer increases Bid to: NT$29,000

Now both sides agree on:

NT$29,000

The transaction can now occur.

After the transaction is completed, NT$29,000 becomes the most recent traded price in our simplified example.

Now Apply the Same Idea to a Stock 📈

Suppose a stock currently has the following best prices:

Simplified Stock Quote
Best Bid
NT$99
Highest current buyer
Best Ask
NT$100
Lowest current seller

This means:

  • The most aggressive buyer is currently willing to pay NT$99.
  • The most aggressive seller is currently willing to sell at NT$100.
  • The Bid–Ask Spread is therefore NT$1.

What If You Want to Buy Immediately?

Suppose the best Bid is NT$99 and the best Ask is NT$100, but you do not want to wait.

You want the stock right now.

If you submit an appropriate marketable buy order, you are effectively accepting the available seller's price.

Buyer wants immediate execution:

Best Ask = NT$100

Therefore, if sufficient shares are available at that price, the buyer may purchase at NT$100.

Similarly, if you already own the stock and want to sell immediately, you may have to accept the current best Bid.

Bid, Ask, and Last Price Are Not the Same Thing

This is a very important beginner distinction.

Term Meaning Example
Bid Highest current price a buyer is willing to pay NT$99
Ask Lowest current price a seller is willing to accept NT$100
Last Price Price of the most recently completed transaction e.g. NT$99.50

Key Idea to Remember

Bid: "What buyers are currently willing to pay."

Ask: "What sellers are currently willing to accept."

Spread: "The difference between the best Ask and best Bid."

Last Price: "The price at which the most recent trade actually occurred."

What Do You Actually Own After Buying a Share?

This deserves special attention because it is often misunderstood.

Suppose you buy shares of a giant semiconductor company.

You cannot walk into one of its factories and say:

"Excuse me, I own shares. That machine in the corner is now mine." 😎

Security will probably have a slightly different interpretation of your ownership.

Owning shares means you have an equity interest in the corporation.

Depending on the type and class of shares, shareholders may have economic and governance rights such as:

  • participating in changes in the company's value,
  • receiving dividends when the company declares and distributes them,
  • voting on certain corporate matters when voting rights apply.

However, you do not personally own a particular desk, factory machine, laptop, building, or coffee machine belonging to the company.

The corporation owns those assets.

You own shares in the corporation.

Why Would Anyone Want to Own a Stock?

Suppose Happy Bubble Tea becomes enormously successful.

It starts with 5 stores.

A few years later, it has 500.

Revenue increases. Profits increase. Customers love the brand.

Investors may now believe the business is worth considerably more than it was previously.

Imagine you purchased shares when the market price was:

NT$20 per share

You purchased:

100 shares

Your initial investment was therefore:

\[ 100 \times NT\$20 = NT\$2,000 \]

Later, suppose the market price rises to:

NT$30 per share

Your 100 shares are now worth:

\[ 100 \times NT\$30 = NT\$3,000 \]

The increase in value is:

\[ NT\$3,000 - NT\$2,000 = NT\$1,000 \] \[ \text{Percentage Increase} = \frac{1,000}{2,000} \times100 = 50\% \]

Nice.

You may now be thinking: "Okay... where has this stock market been all my life?" 😂

But don't celebrate yet.

The exact opposite can happen.

Stocks Can Also Lose Value

Suppose Happy Bubble Tea starts having problems.

  • Customers stop visiting.
  • A competitor becomes more popular.
  • Costs increase.
  • Management makes poor decisions.
  • Profit falls.

Investors may become pessimistic about the company's future.

The share price may fall from:

NT$20 → NT$15

Your 100 shares would now be worth:

\[ 100 \times NT\$15 = NT\$1,500 \]

Compared with your original NT$2,000 investment, the position has fallen by:

\[ NT\$2,000 - NT\$1,500 = NT\$500 \]

A stock can rise, fall, or remain almost unchanged. Profit is never guaranteed.

If somebody tells you:

"This stock will definitely go up."

your beginner brain should already activate its first financial-defense mechanism:

"How do you know?" 🤔

But Why Does the Stock Price Move?

Excellent question.

We will study price movement properly in later chapters, but let us introduce the idea now.

Suppose Happy Bubble Tea announces:

"Our profit increased by 80% this year, and we are opening 100 new stores!"

Many investors may become optimistic.

Suddenly, more people want to buy the shares.

Imagine the conversation:

Buyer A: "I'll pay NT$20."
Buyer B: "I'll pay NT$20.50."
Buyer C: "Give it to me at NT$21!"
Buyer D: "NT$22!"

As buyers compete for available shares, the transaction price can move upward.

Now imagine the opposite announcement:

"Profit dropped 70%, the company has serious debt problems, and 50 stores will close."

Investors may become worried. Many people may try to sell.

If sellers greatly outnumber interested buyers, sellers may need to accept progressively lower prices.

The stock price can therefore fall.

Company Information → Investor Expectations → Buy/Sell Decisions → Price Movement

Real financial markets are considerably more complicated than this.

Prices can also be influenced by:

  • interest rates,
  • inflation,
  • economic growth,
  • industry conditions,
  • government policies,
  • global events,
  • institutional investors,
  • algorithmic trading,
  • fear and greed,
  • and expectations about the future.

But this simplified model is where your intuition should begin.

Company ≠ Stock Price

This is another concept that will become extremely important later.

The company and its stock price are related, but they are not identical things.

The Company 🏢

  • employees
  • factories
  • products
  • technology
  • customers
  • revenue
  • expenses
  • debt
  • cash
  • profits

The Stock Price 📈

The market price represents what market participants are currently willing to pay and accept for ownership in the company.

Sometimes investors become extremely optimistic. The stock price may rise much faster than the underlying business improves.

Sometimes investors become frightened. The stock price may fall even though the company remains profitable.

Therefore, we should never automatically think:

"Price went up = company is definitely good."

or

"Price went down = company is definitely bad."

Later, we will learn how investors study the business through fundamental analysis and how traders study price behavior through technical analysis.

Investor vs Trader: Are They the Same?

You will encounter these two words repeatedly.

📈 Investor

An investor generally focuses more on the business and often holds assets for a longer period.

Typical questions:

  • Is the company profitable?
  • Is revenue growing?
  • Does it have too much debt?
  • Does it have a competitive advantage?
  • Is its current valuation reasonable?
  • Where could the company be in five or ten years?

📊 Trader

A trader generally focuses more on price movement, market structure, timing, and risk.

Typical questions:

  • Is the price trending upward?
  • Where is support?
  • Where is resistance?
  • Is volume increasing?
  • Where should I enter?
  • Where should I exit?
  • How much should I risk?

The distinction is not absolute. Some people invest and trade at the same time, and some strategies fall somewhere between the two.

This tutorial will introduce you to both ways of thinking.

Where Does the Broker Fit In?

You normally cannot simply walk into a stock exchange holding NT$10,000 and announce:

"Hello, one stock please." 😆

Individual investors generally access the market through a brokerage company.

You 👩‍💻   →   Broker 📱   →   Stock Exchange 🏛️   →   Market

You open an account with a broker.

You submit an order.

The broker routes the order into the relevant market infrastructure.

Compatible buying and selling orders may then be matched.

The exchange is the marketplace; the broker is your gateway to it.

Your First Complete Stock-Market Example

Company: Happy Bubble Tea Ltd. 🧋

Total shares: 1,000,000

You buy: 10,000 shares

Your simplified ownership percentage is:

\[ \frac{10,000}{1,000,000} \times100 = 1\% \]

Suppose the share price is: NT$20.

Amount invested:

\[ 10,000 \times NT\$20 = NT\$200,000 \]

Later, the market price becomes: NT$25.

Your shares are now worth:

\[ 10,000 \times NT\$25 = NT\$250,000 \]

The increase in value is:

\[ NT\$250,000-NT\$200,000 = NT\$50,000 \]

Percentage increase:

\[ \frac{50,000}{200,000} \times100 = 25\% \]

But remember: you have not discovered a money-printing machine.

If the stock price had instead fallen to NT$15, your position would have decreased in value by NT$50,000 relative to the purchase price.

Return and risk always travel together.

A Very Important Beginner Mindset

At this stage, your goal should not be:

"Which stock should I buy tomorrow?"

That question comes too early.

Your first questions should be:

1. What am I buying?
2. Why does it have value?
3. Why would somebody want to buy it from me?
4. Why might its price increase or decrease?
5. How much could I lose if my assumption is wrong?

People often enter the stock market obsessed with predicting what will go up.

Risk-aware thinking starts from another question:

"What happens if I am wrong?"

We will return to this question throughout the course.

Quick Summary

  • A company can divide its ownership into shares.
  • A share represents an ownership interest in a corporation.
  • The stock market provides an organized system where securities can be bought and sold.
  • A broker provides investors with access to the market.
  • Stock prices change because buyers and sellers continuously reassess what they are willing to pay or accept.
  • A rising stock can create gains, while a falling stock can create losses.
  • The business itself and its stock price are related, but they are not the same thing.

  • Learning to control losses is eventually just as important as learning to identify opportunities.

Questions that may come to your mind... five minutes after saying "Okay, I understand stocks." 😅

Q1: If I buy one share, am I really an owner of the company?

Yes. A share represents an equity interest in the corporation, although your ownership may be extremely small.

Your exact rights depend on the type and class of shares and the applicable corporate rules.

However, owning one share does not mean you personally own a particular factory, machine, desk, or office chair belonging to the company.

Q2: If I own 1% of a company, can I take 1% of its yearly profit?

Not directly.

The company's profits belong to the corporation. Management and the board may decide how earnings are used.

For example, the company may:

  • reinvest in the business,
  • build new factories,
  • reduce debt,
  • hold additional cash,
  • or distribute part of the earnings as dividends.

Owning shares does not mean you can walk into the accounting department and request your personal 1% envelope. 😆

Q3: If a company is making a lot of profit, will its stock definitely rise?

No.

Financial markets care not only about current results, but also about expectations.

Imagine investors expected the company's profit to increase by 100%, but the company reports an increase of only 30%.

A 30% increase may sound excellent. However, the stock could still fall because the result disappointed market expectations.

Markets often react to the difference between reality and expectation, not simply to whether the news sounds good or bad.

Q4: If my stock price falls, have I immediately lost the money?

The market value of your position has decreased.

If you have not sold the shares, this decrease is commonly described as an unrealized loss.

If you sell below your purchase price, the loss becomes realized.

However, beginners should be careful with the sentence "I haven't sold, so I haven't lost anything." The investment has still decreased in current market value and should be evaluated rationally.

Q5: Is the stock market basically gambling?

It can certainly be used like gambling.

Buying random stocks because someone on social media writes:

"BUY NOW!!! 🚀🚀🚀 TO THE MOON!"

without understanding the company, valuation, strategy, or risk can resemble gambling very closely.

A disciplined investor or trader instead uses:

  • research,
  • predefined rules,
  • risk management,
  • position sizing,
  • diversification where appropriate,
  • and a repeatable decision process.

Uncertainty exists in both cases. The difference lies largely in how that uncertainty is managed.

Q6: Can I become rich quickly through stocks?

Someone can make a very large return over a short period, but achieving unusually large returns usually involves taking unusually large risks.

Therefore, this course is not built around the question:

"How do I turn NT$10,000 into NT$1,000,000 next month?"

Instead, it is built around:

"How do financial markets work, how do I make rational decisions, and how do I protect my capital while learning?"

2. Why Do Companies Issue Shares?

Companies need capital to grow. They may use money to build factories, hire employees, develop products, acquire businesses or strengthen their finances. One way to raise capital is by selling ownership to investors. Investors participate because they hope the business will become more valuable, distribute dividends, or both. That expectation is not guaranteed, which is why investing always involves risk.

3. How Investors Make — and Lose — Money

The simplest source of return is a change in price. If you buy at NT$100 and later sell at NT$120, the gross price gain is NT$20 per share. If the price falls to NT$80, the position has lost NT$20 per share before fees and taxes. Some companies also distribute dividends. Total return therefore can include both price change and cash distributions.

Worked example:

6. Bid, Ask and the Order Book

The bid is a price buyers are offering. The ask is a price sellers are requesting. The difference between them is the bid–ask spread. An order book is like two queues facing each other: buyers line up with prices they will pay, while sellers line up with prices they will accept. Trades occur when compatible orders meet.

7. Market Orders and Limit Orders

In fast markets, execution price can differ from the price you saw moments earlier. A beginner should understand the order before pressing Buy or Sell. Never assume every order behaves the same way.

10. Candlesticks: Open, High, Low, Close

Each candle summarizes four values: Open, High, Low and Close (OHLC). The body shows the relationship between open and close; the wicks show the extremes reached during the period. Candlesticks are a compact language for price action, but one candle should never be treated as a magical prediction.

Epilogue: Your Beginner Checklist

Before considering a real trade, you should be able to answer these questions clearly:

1. What am I buying?
2. Why am I considering it?
3. What is the broader trend and market context?
4. What price or event would prove my idea wrong?
5. How much money could I lose?
6. Is the potential reward reasonable relative to that risk?
7. Am I following a tested rule, or reacting emotionally?
8. Have I accounted for fees, taxes and local market rules?

If you cannot answer these yet, that is a reason to keep learning—not a reason to guess.
